Theory

Two ways to describe where the arm is

Joint space: the configuration q

One number per joint: q = (q₁, q₂, …, qₙ). It pins down the pose of every link. The motors act here, and so do joint limits.

Task space: the hand pose x

Where the end-effector is. In the plane: (x, y). In 3D: position plus orientation, six numbers, an element of the group SE(3). Tasks and obstacles live here.

Two maps connect them

Forward kinematics (FK): q → x. Always one answer, cheap.

Inverse kinematics (IK): x → q. Zero, several or infinitely many answers.

Motion planning mostly happens in joint space, because a point there describes the whole robot, not only its hand.

Forward kinematics: angles to hand position

Given the joint angles, where is the hand? For the two-link arm it is plain trigonometry: add the two link vectors.

x = l₁ cos q₁ + l₂ cos(q₁ + q₂)

y = l₁ sin q₁ + l₂ sin(q₁ + q₂)

A real arm chains one 4×4 homogeneous transform per joint. Each holds a rotation and a translation:

T(q) = T₁(q₁) · T₂(q₂) ⋯ Tₙ(qₙ)

FK always exists, is unique, and costs microseconds. Collision checkers call it constantly to place every link in space.

Inverse kinematics: hand position to angles

Tasks are given for the hand, but motors need joint angles. The inverse is harder: the answer is not unique.

Simple arms have a closed form. Here the law of cosines gives the elbow, and the shoulder follows:

cos q₂ = (x² + y² − l₁² − l₂²) / (2 l₁ l₂)

General arms use numerical IK: iterate from a guess with the Jacobian (next slide). A 6-joint arm can have up to 16 solutions.

Theory · live demo

The Jacobian: joint speed to hand speed

ẋ = J(q) q̇ , J = ∂x / ∂q

Column i of J is the hand velocity when only joint i turns at unit speed.

Numerical IK

Step toward the target with Δq = J⁺ Δx and repeat. J⁺ is the pseudo-inverse; a damped version stays stable near singularities.

Forces

The transpose maps a force F at the hand to joint torques: τ = Jᵀ F. No inverse needed.

Singularities

Where J loses rank, the hand cannot move in some direction and J⁺ blows up. For this arm det J = l₁ l₂ sin q₂, which is zero when the arm is straight or folded.

Part 2 · Motion and control

Theory

Dynamics: torques to motion

Kinematics ignores mass. Dynamics is Newton’s F = ma for a chain of linked bodies, written in joint coordinates. It is called the manipulator equation:

M(q) q̈ + C(q, q̇) q̇ + g(q) = τ

Inertia

M is the mass matrix. It changes with pose: a stretched arm is harder to swing than a folded one.

Coriolis, centrifugal

Velocity coupling. Swinging the shoulder fast throws the elbow outward.

Gravity

The torque needed just to hold still. Zero in orbit; dominant for a slow, heavy arm.

Motor torque τ

What you control. A real robot adds friction and contact forces Jᵀ F on this side.

Forward dynamics

Given torques τ, solve for the acceleration q̈, then integrate. This is what a simulator does every time step (Part 5).

Inverse dynamics

Given a desired acceleration q̈, compute the torques τ. This is what a model-based controller does to follow a trajectory.

P: proportional

A spring pulling toward the target. Stiffer means faster, but too stiff overshoots and oscillates.

Accumulates leftover error and pushes until it is gone. Removes the sag that gravity causes.

A damper resisting fast change of the error. Kills overshoot; amplifies sensor noise if set too high.

Part 2 · Motion and control

Live demo

PID on one joint, three tunings

A single joint lifts a link against gravity to a target of 57° (1 rad). The simulation integrates the joint’s equation of motion with each controller in turn.

P only

Rings for seconds, then settles below the target: the spring must stay stretched to hold the weight.

P + D

The damper removes the ringing. The steady-state sag remains.

P + I + D

The integral term winds up until the sag is cancelled. The joint reaches the target and stays.

In practice arms add a gravity feed-forward term g(q), so the integral has little left to do.

Part 2 · Motion and control

Theory

The motion planning problem

Given the robot’s geometry, the obstacles, a start configuration and a goal,

find a continuous path of configurations from start to goal that never touches an obstacle:

σ : [0, 1] → C_free , σ(0) = q_start , σ(1) = q_goal

Path vs trajectory

A path is geometry only. A trajectory adds timing: where to be at each instant, within speed and torque limits.

Complete

Finds a path whenever one exists and reports failure otherwise. Weaker forms: resolution complete (grids) and probabilistically complete (sampling).

Optimal

Returns the cheapest path, usually the shortest. Asymptotically optimal planners approach it as they run longer.

Hardness

The general problem is PSPACE-hard (Reif, 1979). Exact planners scale exponentially with the number of joints, so practical ones trade guarantees for speed.

Part 3 · Motion planning

Graph search · live demo

A*: search a grid, guided by a heuristic

Cut the configuration space into cells. Free neighbouring cells are connected. Always expand the open cell with the lowest score:

f(n) = g(n) + h(n)

g: cost paid from the start. h: estimated cost left to the goal.

h = 0: Dijkstra

No guidance. The search spreads evenly in every direction until it happens to reach the goal.

h = distance to goal: A*

If h never overestimates (it is “admissible”), the path is still the shortest, and far fewer cells are expanded.

Why grids stop working

Cells multiply with every joint

Divide each joint’s range into 100 steps. The grid then has 100ⁿ cells for n joints.

2 joints

10⁴

Ten thousand cells. A* finishes in milliseconds.

3 joints

10⁶

A million. Still fine.

6 joints

10¹²

A trillion. More than fits in memory.

7 joints

10¹⁴

At a million collision checks per second, visiting every cell once takes about three years.

Sampling-based planners sidestep this. They never build the grid; they only probe it at random points.

Part 3 · Motion planning

Sampling · live demo

PRM: build a roadmap once, query it often

The probabilistic roadmap (Kavraki et al., 1996) never represents the obstacles explicitly. It only asks a collision checker “is this configuration free?”

1 · Sample

Draw random configurations. Keep the collision-free ones as nodes.

2 · Connect

Join each node to its nearest neighbours when the straight segment between them is free.

3 · Query

Attach start and goal to the graph and run A* or Dijkstra on it.

Best when the scene is static and many queries follow, as in a fixed work cell. With too few samples the roadmap misses narrow gaps and the query fails.

Part 3 · Motion planning

Sampling · live demo

RRT: grow a tree toward random samples

The rapidly-exploring random tree (LaValle, 1998) answers one query with no roadmap.

repeat
  q_rand ← random configuration
  q_near ← nearest node in the tree
  q_new  ← one step from q_near
           toward q_rand
  if segment is collision-free
    add q_new to the tree
until q_new reaches the goal

Why it explores fast. Frontier nodes border the largest empty regions, so random samples usually pull the tree outward.

RRT-Connect grows one tree from each end until they meet. It is the default planner in MoveIt.

Part 3 · Motion planning

Sampling · live demo

RRT*: keep improving after the first path

RRT stops at its first path, which is usually crooked. RRT* (Karaman and Frazzoli, 2011) adds two steps for every new node:

Choose the best parent

Among all tree nodes within a radius, attach to the one giving the lowest total cost from the start.

Rewire the neighbours

If routing a nearby node through the new one is cheaper, change that node’s parent.

Asymptotic optimality

As the number of samples grows, the path cost converges to the optimum with probability 1. You choose when to stop. Informed RRT* and BIT* get there faster.

Part 3 · Motion planning

Optimization · live demo

Trajectory optimization: bend a guess into shape

Represent the whole motion as a chain of waypoints q₀ … q_T. Start from any guess, even one that collides, and descend a cost:

J = Σ ‖qₜ₊₁ − qₜ‖² + λ Σ c_obs(qₜ)

First term: smoothness. Second term: penalty that grows as a waypoint nears an obstacle.

CHOMP follows the gradient using a distance field. STOMP samples noisy variations instead of gradients. TrajOpt treats collisions as hard constraints in a sequence of convex problems.

The catch: local optima

The demo alternates two initial guesses. Each converges, but to a different path with a different cost. Nothing tells the optimizer that a better route exists.

Part 3 · Motion planning

Theory

From path to trajectory: adding the clock

A path has no timing. Time parameterization assigns a time to every point without exceeding the robot’s limits.

Trapezoidal

Accelerate at the limit, cruise at top speed, decelerate. Simple and common. Acceleration jumps, so the motion can jerk.

Polynomial, S-curve

Cubic or quintic splines through the waypoints. Acceleration is continuous, so motion is smoother and gentler on the gears.

Time-optimal

TOPP-RA finds the fastest timing along a fixed path under velocity, acceleration and torque limits.

Why it matters for learning

Learned policies output sparse waypoints. The same interpolation and limit checks run underneath.

Reactive · live demo

Potential fields: roll downhill to the goal

Skip planning. Pretend the goal attracts the robot and each obstacle repels it (Khatib, 1986), then follow the combined force every tick.

U(q) = U_goal(q) + Σ U_obs(q) , q̇ = −∇U(q)

Scene A

Scattered obstacles. The robot slides around them and arrives. Cost: one gradient per tick.

Scene B

A U-shaped wall. Attraction and repulsion cancel inside the cup: a local minimum. The robot stops short.

Hence the usual pairing: a global planner for the route, a reactive layer for surprises. MPC sits in between, re-optimizing a short horizon every tick.

Part 4 · Robot learning

Theory

A policy maps observations to actions

Observation oₜ

images, joint angles, instruction

Policy π_θ

a neural network with weights θ

Action aₜ

targets for the low-level controller

Robot and world

produce the next observation

The loop repeats 10 to 50 times per second. Formally: aₜ ~ π_θ(a | oₜ), a Markov decision process seen through partial observations (a POMDP).

What goes in

One or more camera views, often a wrist camera plus a fixed one. Proprioception: joint angles and gripper opening. Sometimes a language instruction and a short history.

What comes out

Joint position targets, or a small change of the hand pose plus gripper open or close. Torques are common only in simulation RL. The choice of action space affects how easy learning is.

Action chunking

Predict the next H actions at once (H of 8 to 100), execute some of them, then predict again. Chunks give smoother motion and fewer decision points where errors can enter.

Part 4 · Robot learning

Imitation learning

Behaviour cloning: copy the demonstrator

A person teleoperates the robot and every step is logged as a pair (observation, action). Training is then ordinary supervised learning:

L(θ) = E ‖ π_θ(o) − a ‖²

The catch: compounding error

Supervised learning assumes test inputs look like training inputs. Here the policy’s own actions decide its next input. One small error leads to a state no demo visited, where the next prediction is worse. Over T steps the error can grow like T², not T (Ross and Bagnell, 2010).

Remedies

DAgger: let the policy drive, have the expert relabel the states it reaches, retrain. Demonstrations that include recoveries. Action chunking. Much more, and more varied, data.

Imitation learning

Why plain regression averages into the obstacle

half the demos go over

half go under

their average

Both ways around are correct. The mean of two correct answers is a collision.

Minimizing squared error makes the network output the average action for each observation, E[a | o]. Human demonstrations are multimodal, so the average can be an action nobody ever took.

The fix: model the whole distribution p(a | o), then sample from it

Discrete tokens

Bin each action dimension and classify. RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA.

Latent variable

A CVAE picks a “style”, a transformer decodes a chunk. ACT.

Diffusion

Denoise random noise into an action chunk. Diffusion Policy.

Flow matching

Same idea along straighter paths, so fewer steps. π0, SmolVLA.

Part 4 · Robot learning

Generative policies · live demo

Diffusion policy: denoise noise into actions

Training

Take a demonstrated action chunk a⁰. Corrupt it with Gaussian noise ε at a random level k. Train the network to recover the noise, given the observation o:

L = E ‖ ε − ε_θ(aᵏ, k, o) ‖²

Acting

Draw pure noise. Apply the network K times (10 to 100), removing a little noise each time. Execute the first few actions of the result, observe, repeat.

Why it suits robots

Different noise draws settle into different modes, so nothing is averaged. Training is a stable regression, and whole action sequences come out smooth.

Reinforcement learning

RL: learn from trial, error and reward

Agent

policy π_θ

Environment

robot in simulation

action aₜ

No demonstrations. The agent acts, sees a reward, and adjusts.

Objective: maximize expected discounted return

J(θ) = E [ Σₜ γᵗ rₜ ]

Policy gradient: make better-than-expected actions more likely

∇J = E [ ∇ log π_θ(aₜ | sₜ) · Âₜ ]

Two algorithms to know

PPO: on-policy, robust, hungry for samples. The standard with thousands of parallel simulated robots. SAC: off-policy with a replay buffer, far more sample-efficient. Add hindsight relabelling (HER) for goal-reaching.

Reward design

A sparse reward (“1 if the cube is lifted”) is honest but gives little signal. A shaped reward guides learning but invites loopholes the agent will find.

Sample cost

Millions to billions of steps. Too slow and too risky on hardware, so training happens in simulation and transfers afterwards: next slide.

Where it wins

Dynamic, contact-rich skills that are hard to demonstrate: legged locomotion, in-hand manipulation, agile flight.

A simulator is always a little wrong: friction, motor delay, gear backlash, camera noise, lighting. A policy can exploit those errors and then fail on hardware.

Domain randomization

Vary physics and visuals every episode, so reality looks like one more variation.

System identification

Measure the real robot and tune the simulator to match it.

Teacher and student

A teacher policy trains with privileged simulator state. A student then learns to imitate it from realistic sensors only.

Real data in the loop

Fine-tune on a little real experience, or rebuild real scenes as simulation assets.

Why start from a VLM

Robot data is scarce. A model pretrained on web images and text already knows what a “cup” looks like, so robot data only has to teach it how to move.

Two kinds of action head

Tokens: bin each action dimension and emit it like text (RT-2, OpenVLA). Flow or diffusion: a separate head generates continuous chunks (π0, GR00T N1, SmolVLA).

Data

Open X-Embodiment: over a million trajectories from 22 robot types. DROID: 76 thousand trajectories in varied real scenes. Then a few dozen demos to fine-tune for your robot.

Size and speed

SmolVLA 0.45 B parameters, π0 about 3.3 B, OpenVLA 7 B. The control loop has a deadline, so chunking, quantization and fast inference decide what can run on a robot.

Theory

What a physics simulator computes each step

1 · Apply controls

Your code writes motor commands. Actuator models turn them into joint torques τ.

2 · Detect collisions

Find every pair of touching shapes, with contact points and normals.

3 · Solve dynamics

Forward dynamics with contact forces as constraints: solve for the accelerations q̈.

4 · Integrate

Advance velocities and positions by one small time step Δt.

5 · Render sensors

Report joint angles, forces and camera images for your code to read.

q̈ = M(q)⁻¹ ( τ + Jᵀ f_contact − C(q, q̇) q̇ − g(q) ) , q̇ ← q̇ + Δt q̈ , q ← q + Δt q̇

The manipulator equation from slide 11, solved forward, then one semi-implicit Euler step. Repeated 200 to 1000 times per simulated second.

The time step

Typically 1 to 5 ms. Larger steps run faster but stiff contacts go unstable. A policy at 20 Hz acts once every 10 to 50 physics steps.

Contact is the hard part

Rigid bodies cannot overlap, so forces switch on abruptly. Each engine softens or solves this differently, and that choice is a main source of the reality gap.

Robot description files

URDF is the common exchange format: links, joints, meshes, inertias. MuJoCo’s own MJCF adds actuators, sensors and contact settings. Lab 0 writes one by hand.

Lab 1 · numpy

Lab 1: kinematics in twenty lines

import numpy as np
L1, L2 = 0.4, 0.3  # link lengths (metres)

def fk(q):  # joint angles -> hand (x, y)
    a, b = q[0], q[0] + q[1]
    return np.array([L1*np.cos(a) + L2*np.cos(b), L1*np.sin(a) + L2*np.sin(b)])

def jac(q):  # d(hand) / d(joints), 2 x 2
    a, b = q[0], q[0] + q[1]
    return np.array([[-L1*np.sin(a) - L2*np.sin(b), -L2*np.sin(b)],
                     [ L1*np.cos(a) + L2*np.cos(b),  L2*np.cos(b)]])

def ik(target, q=np.array([0.3, 0.3]), damp=1e-2):
    for _ in range(100):  # damped least squares
        J, err = jac(q), target - fk(q)
        q = q + J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + damp*np.eye(2), err)
    return q

if __name__ == "__main__":
    q = ik(np.array([0.5, 0.2])); print(q.round(3), fk(q).round(3))

Lab 2 · numpy

Lab 2: an RRT in the arm’s joint space

import numpy as np
from lab1 import fk, L1
rng, OBS = np.random.default_rng(0), np.array([0.45, 0.35, 0.12])  # x, y, radius
def collides(q):  # sample points on both links
    elbow = L1 * np.array([np.cos(q[0]), np.sin(q[0])])
    pts = np.vstack([np.linspace([0, 0], elbow, 8), np.linspace(elbow, fk(q), 8)])
    return np.linalg.norm(pts - OBS[:2], axis=1).min() < OBS[2]

def rrt(start, goal, step=0.15):
    nodes, parent = [np.array(start)], [0]
    while np.linalg.norm(nodes[-1] - goal) > step:
        target = goal if rng.random() < 0.1 else rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi, 2)
        i = int(np.argmin([np.linalg.norm(target - n) for n in nodes]))
        d = target - nodes[i]
        new = nodes[i] + d * min(1.0, step / np.linalg.norm(d))
        if not collides(new): nodes.append(new); parent.append(i)
    path = [len(nodes) - 1]  # walk back through parents
    while path[-1] != 0: path.append(parent[path[-1]])
    return [nodes[j] for j in path[::-1]]
print(len(rrt([-0.5, 0.5], np.array([1.8, -0.4]))), "waypoints")

Lab 3 · mujoco

Lab 3: closed-loop reaching on a physics engine

import mujoco, numpy as np

m = mujoco.MjModel.from_xml_path("arm.xml")
d = mujoco.MjData(m)
tip = m.site("tip").id
target = np.array([0.35, 0.40])  # where the tip should go (m)
jacp = np.zeros((3, m.nv))  # 3 x nv position Jacobian

mujoco.mj_forward(m, d)  # compute poses once
for step in range(2000):  # 4 s of simulated time
    err = target - d.site_xpos[tip][:2]
    mujoco.mj_jacSite(m, d, jacp, None, tip)
    J = jacp[:2]  # planar arm: x and y rows
    dq = J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + 1e-3*np.eye(2), err)
    d.ctrl[:] = d.qpos + 0.5 * dq  # nudge the joint targets
    mujoco.mj_step(m, d)  # physics does the rest
    if step % 400 == 0: print(step, "error (m):", np.linalg.norm(err).round(4))

print("final tip:", d.site_xpos[tip][:2].round(3), "joints:", d.qpos.round(3))

Lab 4 · torch

Lab 4: clone a controller, then roll it out

import numpy as np, torch, torch.nn as nn
from lab1 import fk, jac
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
T = lambda v: torch.tensor(np.array(v), dtype=torch.float32)
def expert(q, goal):  # one damped IK step (Lab 1)
    J = jac(q)
    return 0.3 * J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + 1e-2*np.eye(2), goal - fk(q))
obs, act = [], []  # 1. collect demonstrations
for _ in range(2000):
    q, goal = rng.uniform(-2, 2, 2), fk(rng.uniform(-2, 2, 2))
    for _ in range(20):
        a = expert(q, goal); obs.append(np.r_[q, goal]); act.append(a); q = q + a
X, Y = T(obs), T(act)
pi = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(4, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, 2))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(pi.parameters(), 1e-3)
for epoch in range(500):  # 2. supervised regression
    loss = ((pi(X) - Y) ** 2).mean(); opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
q, goal = np.array([0.3, 0.3]), np.array([0.5, 0.2])  # 3. roll out the clone
for _ in range(40): q = q + pi(T(np.r_[q, goal])).detach().numpy()
print("loss", loss.item(), "tip error (m)", np.linalg.norm(fk(q) - goal))

Goal. Slide 29 as code. The expert is Lab 1’s IK step; the policy sees (q, goal) and outputs Δq.

Expect. Loss about 0.02. The clone stops 1 to 2 cm from the goal; the expert reaches it exactly.

Exercises

Check yourself: motion, control and planning

1 · Kinematics

With l₁ = 0.4 m and l₂ = 0.3 m, where is the hand at q = (90°, −90°)?

Link 1 points up, link 2 points along x. The hand is at (0.3, 0.4) m.

2 · Inverse kinematics

Which targets of that arm have exactly one IK solution?

Those exactly 0.7 m or 0.1 m from the base: arm fully straight or fully folded. These are its singularities.

3 · Control

A P-only joint controller sags under gravity. You double Kp. What changes?

The steady error halves. The damping ratio drops, so overshoot and ringing grow.

4 · Search vs sampling

Why is grid A* hopeless for a 7-joint arm while RRT is not?

The grid has about 10¹⁴ cells. RRT never enumerates the space: it samples points and collision-checks them.

5 · Path quality

RRT returned a path. Is it the shortest? What next?

No, only feasible. Shortcut and smooth it, hand it to a trajectory optimizer, or run RRT* for longer.

6 · Optimization

A trajectory optimizer keeps converging to a path through an obstacle. Two fixes?

Seed it with a path from a sampling planner. Or restart from several random guesses and keep the best.

Cover the grey answer, decide, then compare. Questions 1 and 2 can be checked with Lab 1.

Part 6 · Hands-on

Exercises

Check yourself: learning and simulation

1 · Behaviour cloning

A cloned policy has low validation loss but fails when rolled out. Why?

Validation uses states the expert visited. In a rollout the policy’s own errors lead to unseen states, where errors compound.

2 · Multimodality

Demos pass an obstacle on either side. What does an MSE-trained policy do?

It outputs the average and drives at the obstacle. Model the distribution instead: diffusion, flow, tokens or a CVAE.

3 · Diffusion policy

Sampling takes tens of network calls. How can it control at 10 Hz or more?

Action chunking: one sample yields many future actions, and several run before re-sampling. Fewer-step samplers help too.

4 · Sim-to-real

A policy trained in simulation shakes on the real robot. Three likely causes?

Actuator delay, wrong friction or damping, sensor noise. Randomize them in training, or measure them and fix the model.

5 · Simulation

The physics step is 2 ms and the policy runs at 25 Hz. How many steps per action?

One action lasts 40 ms, so 20 physics steps.

6 · Judgement

When is a classical planner the better choice than a learned policy?

Known geometry, a need for guarantees, tasks that differ only by goal, and no data: a typical industrial cell.

Question 1 is what Lab 4 demonstrates when you cut the number of demonstrations.
